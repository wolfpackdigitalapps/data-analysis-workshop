Data Analysis Workshop — Rubric

One page of guidance per exercise: what a strong answer looks like, where pairs usually go wrong, and the questions to push on. All figures refer to the demo product Nimbus (synthetic data).

1

Map the user journey

10 min + 5 debrief
Turn the product into stages with a human lens, and form a hypothesis before touching data.

What good looks like

  • Stages run first-touch → repeat use; each row has a real user goal (not a feature), action, touchpoint, emotion (1–5).
  • Gates (Verify ID, Link bank) are marked; the lowest-emotion stage is usually a gate.
  • A falsifiable hypothesis: "biggest drop at ___ because ___". Strong pairs predict verification from feeling the app.
  • The journey runs all the way to coming back — six stages, not five. This is now the highest-leverage thing you grade, because Exercise 2's funnel is built automatically from whatever they draw here. A journey that stops at the first payment produces a funnel that stops at the first payment, and the pair never sees that 3,360 of 6,720 come back — a 50% repeat rate, the one number in the whole funnel that says the product works for the people who reach it. Carried one stage further, only 6.7% of installs come back. A step they do not draw is a step they will never see losing users.
  • Know what the workbook gives them: one blank card. Building the journey is the exercise now — nothing is pre-filled, including the stage names. So do not grade "did they fill the boxes"; grade the shape of the map: does it start before the install, does it end after the user has come back, are the mandatory steps marked, and does the emotion line dip where a human would flinch.

Common misses

  • Listing screens/features instead of what the user is trying to do.
  • Skipping emotion, or not marking which steps are mandatory gates.
  • A vague hypothesis ("onboarding is bad") with no specific step.
  • A three- or four-stage journey. The commonest failure by far, and the most expensive: it silently caps what Exercise 2 can find. Catch it here, in the room, not in Exercise 2 — by then they have typed numbers into a funnel that cannot show them the bottleneck.

Push here

  • "What does the user actually want at this stage — in their words?" · "Which steps are mandatory gates?"
  • "Walk me from never heard of it to used it twice. Where does your map start, and where does it stop?" Anything shorter than six stages, send them back before the timer ends.
  • Insist on a specific, checkable hypothesis before Exercise 2. Write each pair's predicted drop-stage on the board.
2

Build the funnel from the data

10 min + 5 debrief
Measure the journey they drew, and locate its weakest step by the numbers.

What good looks like

  • The funnel is their own journey, row for row. The workbook builds it from Exercise 1 automatically, and the two pages are the same list seen from two ends — renaming, reordering, adding (＋) or deleting (✕) a row here changes their journey too, so a pair who spots a missing step while staring at the numbers can add it without going back and retyping anything. What they owe each row is two things: which dashboard event counts it, and the user count read off Nimbus Analytics → Funnels.
  • Counts transcribed correctly; step conversion computed by hand (this ÷ previous).
  • Identifies sign-up → verify identity (40%) as the worst step — in their stage words, which is how the sheet will name it back to them; notes it is also the biggest absolute loss (~19,200/mo).
  • Computes overall install→first-transaction ≈ 13.4%, and one stage further to repeat ≈ 6.7%, and sizes the prize of fixing the weak step. "Check my math" prints the lost-user count for whichever step they flag, so the prize is on screen — make them say what it is worth before they move on.
  • Every event mapped, and the sheet says so. After the arithmetic verdict, "Check my math" checks their map against the instrument and names any dashboard event no stage claims. A pair that reads "Every event the dashboard reports has a stage in your journey ✓" has done Exercise 1 properly. A pair that reads "your journey has no stage for Repeat (30d)" has just been handed the finding — see that they go back and add it rather than shrugging.

Common misses

  • Reading the % off the screen instead of computing it.
  • Confusing step vs overall conversion; flagging install→signup (64%) or a big-count step.
  • Arithmetic slips — have them use the workbook's "Check my math".
  • Leaving the "measured by…" boxes empty and typing counts against their own labels. That is the sloppy habit the whole day is against: a number is only a number of something. Unmapped rows are listed by name in the verdict.
  • Two stages claiming the same event, or stages running in a different order than the dashboard reports them. Both are flagged, and both are worth stopping on — an out-of-order map is a claim about the product that the product does not support.

Push here

  • "Compare each step to its neighbours." · "Which loses the most users, and which has the worst rate?"
  • "If this step went to 60%, what's the prize?" Celebrate pairs who called it from the app in Ex 1.
  • To a pair with a short funnel: "The dashboard reports six events. Your funnel has four rows. Which two steps are your users going through that your map is silent about?" They can add them without leaving the page — ＋ on any funnel row inserts a stage after it, and it appears in their Exercise 1 journey blank, waiting to be described. Numbers travel with the stage, so nothing they have typed is lost either way. The stage arrives unnamed on purpose: the funnel tells you a step exists, and only the journey can tell you what the user was trying to do there.
3

Diagnose the bottleneck

18 min + 7 debrief · protect this time
Localize the leak by segment, then explain why with the voice of the customer. Triangulate.

What good looks like

  • Segments the KYC step: Android 30% vs iOS 52%, paid social 28%, driver's licence 28%; narrows to ID photo capture (61%) of everyone who abandons.
  • Builds the segment rather than scanning for it. A one-dimension gap is a candidate; the test is holding the other dimension still. On the dashboard that is + Add comparison → pick a dimension and value, then ＋ and… to AND a second condition into the same chip — one group, defined once, and every report splits by it until it is cleared. Three outcomes, all legitimate: the gap survives (the platform gap does), dissolves, or reverses — and a reversal is the most valuable thing that can happen to a pair, because it means the story they walked in with was backwards.
  • Catches the country confounder — the trap the data is built around. Romania converts at 41.8% and Moldova at 33.5%, which reads as a market problem worth a market response. Build Android AND Moldova against Android AND Romania and the two land at 29.2% and 30.2%: an 8.3-point gap collapses to about one point, well inside the margin at those sample sizes. Moldova is not worse at KYC; Moldova is more Android. A pair that reports the headline gap as a finding did the arithmetic correctly and the analysis wrongly, and that distinction is the single most transferable thing they take home.
  • Rules out the confounder before believing the platform story. Document type has the wider spread (58/40/28 against 52/30), so the honest first question is whether Android simply attracts worse documents. Cross platform by ID type and it does not: standardising Android to the iOS document mix moves it from 30.1% to only 30.7%, so the document mix explains 0.7 of the 21.9 points. A finding that survives its own strongest objection is a diagnosis; one that was never tested is a correlation.
  • Reads a cross-tab against what the two margins already predict. With no interaction, a cell is just row × column ÷ overall. "Android + paid social 22%" is the classic false catch — those two margins predict 21.0%, so the cell is very slightly better than expected and there is nothing in it. The real interaction sits in the document cross-tab: driver's licences on Android convert at 17.0% against 21.0% predicted, while on iOS they beat prediction at 42.1% against 36.4%. The same document moving opposite ways on the two platforms is a capture problem, and it points straight at the camera.
  • Asks for n before believing a gap. The workbook prints a live 95% margin as they type the two rates and the sample size: at n≈180 that margin is ±6.7 points, so a five-point difference is a coin flip; at n≈9,000 it is ±0.9 and the same five points is real. A gap smaller than its own margin is a null result, not a small effect — and a null result honestly reported is a pass, not a failure.
  • ≥3 pieces of evidence, and at least one of them not analytics (funnel + segment + reviews/tickets/NPS). Funnel, segments and Flows are three views of one event stream; the users' own words are the separate witness.
  • Bonus, and rare: settles the tracking question inside the funnel before blaming the UX. An event that under-fired at verification would inflate every rate measured after it — and nothing downstream is inflated; the later steps track iOS within about 2pp. Better still, split verification into its two halves (reach the ID-photo screen, then clear approval): they multiply back to the step itself, so the gap divides between them exactly, and 97% of it opens after the photo is sent. They are not giving up before they try — they try and are turned down.
  • The ceiling of the exercise, and nobody reaches it by accident: cuts the platform gap by device model, then by app version, and finds a regression. "Android" is not one population. On 4.2.9 the budget handsets — Samsung A-series and Xiaomi Redmi, 4,474 sign-ups — verified at 35.8%, indistinguishable from Android's flagships at 35.9%. From 4.3.0 they collapse to 14.1% and stay down through 4.3.1, while flagships (36.0%, 36.1%) and iPhones (52.0% on all three versions) never move. A release broke ID capture on low-end Android hardware and the release after it did not fix it. That is 4,671 sign-ups converting at 14.0% where they used to convert at 35.8%: 1,015 verified users, 3.2 points of the 20-point gap, and Android as a platform goes 30.1% → 35.9% the week it is reverted.
  • Then separates the release from the calendar — the same discipline a third time, and the part that makes it a finding instead of a coincidence. Sign-up week shows the identical collapse (43.1% the week of Jun 30, 37.6% the week of Jul 7) because the rollout and the calendar are the same rows read two ways. A pair that stops at "something happened in early July" has a date. A pair that notices flagship Android does not move across those same weeks has ruled out everything that would hit all handsets equally — a traffic-mix shift, a campaign, a seasonal effect — and is left with the one explanation that only touches the devices running the new code path. Hold the week still and the version still matters; hold the device still and the week stops mattering.
  • Names a hypothesis it killed. The workbook will not mark Exercise 3 complete without one. A diagnosis with no casualties almost always means nothing was actually tested — the pair went looking for confirmation and found it.
  • One-sentence diagnosis naming a cause (fragile ID-capture UX + Android camera), not a symptom. The best version of that sentence names two causes with different price tags: a structural capture weakness on Android that predates every release in this data and has to be built out of, and a 4.3.0 regression on budget handsets that only has to be reverted. Most rooms find the first. A pair that finds both has stopped producing a diagnosis and started producing a decision.

Common misses

  • Pointing at the red bar and stopping (in Hard mode there's no red bar — good).
  • "Onboarding is bad" = a symptom. Blaming users. A single source of evidence.
  • Counting three charts as three independent sources. The funnel, the segment split and Flows all read the same events — if the Android event misfires, all three agree and all three are wrong.
  • Reporting the aggregate gap after their own build dissolved it. This is the one to watch for now. A pair finds Moldova at 33.5% against Romania's 41.8%, builds Android AND Moldova versus Android AND Romania, sees 29.2% and 30.2% — and still writes "Moldova underperforms" in the diagnosis box, because the headline number was the one they had already decided to believe. The build was not the mistake; ignoring it was.
  • Quoting a gap with no n underneath it. "Referral converts 6 points better" is not a finding until you know whether it was measured on 180 people or 9,000. At the small end that gap is inside the margin and the honest report is we could not tell. Pairs treat "no difference" as a failed exercise; it is the correct answer surprisingly often.
  • Stacking conditions until the segment is too small to say anything. The opposite failure, and it looks like rigour: four ANDed conditions, n=40, a 15-point gap and a margin of ±15. Every extra condition buys precision about the group and pays for it in certainty about the number.
  • Treating "Android" as one population — the most expensive simplification available in this dataset. On the versions users are actually running it conceals a 22-point spread between handsets on the same OS (budget 14.0%, flagship 36.0%), and it folds a two-day revert into a twelve-week rebuild where nobody will ever cost it separately. The pair is not wrong; they have stopped one cut early, and the cut they stopped before is the one that changes what ships on Monday. When a diagnosis ends in a platform name, ask what is inside the platform.

Push here

  • "Give me three receipts, and one of them can't be a chart." · "Is that a cause or a symptom?" · "Why does that screen fail?"
  • "Build it — hold one thing still." The single most useful instruction of the day. Any pair reading a one-dimension gap off the segment table gets it: add the second condition to the chip, then tell me whether the gap survived, dissolved or reversed. Do not accept "it looks like" — make them define the group, read the two numbers and say which of the three happened.
  • "How big is that group, and what is the margin?" Ask it every single time, especially when you agree with the conclusion. The habit only sticks if it is applied to findings the room likes.
  • In the debrief: "What did you believe at minute five that you no longer believe?" A pair with no answer did not test anything. Ask it of the room before you show the answer key — the pair that admits the country story fell apart under their own build should get the loudest response of the session.
  • "How do you know the tracking is right?" — the innocent explanation for an Android gap is a broken event. Make them answer it from the funnel first (a missing event inflates what comes after it; nothing after it is inflated), then bring the reviews in as the independent second witness. A pair that answers with the reviews alone has learned the wrong reflex — the reviews corroborate the finding, they are not the test of it.
  • "Is Android one thing?" — the highest-yield question of the day, and almost nobody asks it unprompted. Hold it back until a pair has already proved the platform gap and is writing it up; asking it early replaces their analysis with yours. The two dimensions are sitting on the dashboard (Device model, App version) and what they lead to is a genuine regression rather than a UX opinion. If they get there, the follow-up is the real question: "would you still recommend the rebuild?" The answer that earns the loudest response in the room is yes — and I would ship the revert first, because it is days rather than a quarter and it moves the baseline everything else is measured against.
  • Grade on triangulation, not on naming KYC. Turn on the dashboard's Hard mode so they do the work.
4

Build the initiative-based plan

22 min + 8 debrief · centrepiece
Objective → Initiatives → Actions. Bets tied to evidence, prioritised, adding up to the target.

What good looks like

  • One Objective (KPI, baseline 40%, target 60%, date). 3–4 initiatives, each tied to specific Ex-3 evidence.
  • Each has a hypothesis, 2–4 actions, expected pp, effort; ICE-scored & ranked (ID-capture #1).
  • Expected lifts cover the 20-pt gap — and the pair says out loud that the sum is a ceiling, not a forecast, because overlapping bets rescue the same user. Names a guardrail and the value at which they would stop; the tracker and the workbook both have a field for it, and the model answer is on-time advance repayment, 94% today (4,295 of 4,570), stop and reassess below 92% — a number already on the dashboard, under Cohorts, which is also the number that moves if you loosen the gate to hit the target.
  • Expect the sum to land on exactly 20, and do not let it pass. Ours does — 10 + 5 + 3 + 2 against a 20-point gap — and the tracker flags its own example for it, because a total that lands precisely on the target is the signature of a number worked backwards from the target. The answer is not to fiddle the estimates: it is to size the strongest bet on its own ("ID-capture, +10pp, and here is why") and offer the other three as headroom. One bet you can defend beats four that sum to the answer.
  • Says whose rate it is before it says what a point is worth. "+1pp" is meaningless until you say a point of what, measured on whom, and the money moves with the denominator exactly: all 32,000 sign-ups is $884/mo at plateau, the 17,500 Android sign-ups is $483, the 5,400 who upload a driving licence on Android is $149. 5.9× across that ladder, behind three identical-looking "+1pp"s — and that is not a rounding error, it is the difference between a bet that clears its cost and one that does not. Credit the pair whose scope and whose money are the same population. The workbook nudges but does not block. It flags an unscoped plan in amber ("this money has no population attached to it") and calls out a value-per-point more than 25% off what the stated scope implies, and it refuses outright only when the scope is bigger than the whole funnel. Everything else still prices. So this one is caught by you, not by the tool: walk the room at minute 12 of Exercise 4, when the middle box is filled and the pitch is not yet written, and ask one question at each table — "a point of what, measured on whom?"
  • The plan has a price on it. Person-weeks on every bet, one defensible number for what a point of the KPI is worth per month, and a payback in months. On Nimbus: 37 person-weeks ≈ $148k at $4,000 loaded per person-week, against $884 per point per month, paying back in about 10 months — $1.33 back per $1 in year one. Best answer puts the ICE order and the payback order side by side and says which one it is following, and why. On Nimbus the two agree — ID-capture → soft-fail → Android → recover, both ways — so do not fish for a disagreement that is not there; the agreement is the finding. What it does not mean is that costing the plan told you nothing. ICE ranks the four; only the money says whether the fourth should be built at all — recover abandoners is last on both lists, and payback adds the fact ICE cannot see: $20k for +2pp returns $0.98 per $1 in year one and pays back in month 13, so it never washes its face inside the year. A ranking gives you an order, not a verdict on inclusion. That single row is the whole case for putting a price on a plan.
  • Ranking and sequencing are two different decisions. Even when the orders agree, what you ship first can differ from what scores highest — and the model client deck ships nothing on its own ICE list first. Week zero is the 4.3.0 revert, which is not a bet, has no ICE score and is not inside the 37 person-weeks; the first build win is initiative #2 (soft-fail + pre-KYC checklist, 6 weeks) in week two, while the 14-week rebuild is still in flight. That is the shape to credit: a delivery order that is argued from cost and time-to-value, not read off the ranking. Press a pair that assumes rank order is a delivery plan — and press one that leaves the revert out of the plan entirely, because a free win omitted is the same error as a bet unpriced.
  • Best in the room, and almost nobody gets there: notices that the biggest single lever on the board is not a product fix at all. Paid social is 12,400 of the 32,000 sign-ups and converts at 28%; every other channel put together is 9,328 of 19,600 = 47.6%. Move that volume to the rest's blended rate and it is +2,429 verified, or +7.6pp of a 20-point gap — bigger than any bet in our plan, and every bet in our plan is an engineering project. The honest version is the better half of the lesson: the two best channels (Referral 52%, Organic 50%) cannot be bought. The best channel you can buy is Paid search at 35.2%, worth about +2.8pp, from a base of 2,400 that would not hold its rate if you scaled it six-fold. So the finding survives, smaller and real: bring the spend question into the room, then make them say what is actually purchasable.
  • Prices the regression apart from the rebuild. A pair that found the 4.3.0 collapse in Exercise 3 is holding the only bet on the board with no engineering quarter attached to it: reverting one capture path on two handset families, days of work, worth about 3.2 of the 20 points on its own — roughly $2,800 a month at the all-users $884 a point, against 37 person-weeks for everything else. It is the whole argument for costing bets instead of only ranking them, because ICE has no way to say "this one is free". Two consequences to press for. It ships first regardless of where it ranks — which is exactly why the model client deck puts it in week zero, ahead of everything on its own ICE list. And it is the cleanest example of the overlap lesson: a rebuilt capture flow would eventually have rescued most of those same users, so the revert's 3.2 points and bet ①'s 10 do not add — the revert simply gets them back in a week, and bet ① is worth less than its headline once it has. A plan that claims both in full has double-counted, and this is the one place in the exercise where the double-count is easy to see.
  • Also strong: spots the parity trap — Android at full iOS parity is still only 52% blended (9,100 + 7,540 of 32,000), so 60% needs roughly +20pp on both platforms. That is why bet ① is a rebuild for everyone, not an Android patch.

Common misses

  • Solutions with no evidence link; scope creep ("redesign onboarding").
  • Lifts don't add to the target (a wishlist). ICE gamed. No guardrail. No experiment/measurement plan.
  • Lifts that add to exactly the gap. That is usually a number reverse-engineered from the target rather than estimated toward it — press on it.
  • Treating this as an Android fix while quietly assuming iOS holds still.
  • No cost anywhere. Effort as a T-shirt size is not a cost — nobody has ever approved a budget denominated in "M". If a pair cannot say what a point of their KPI is worth, that is the finding: send them to get it before they pitch.
  • A scoped diagnosis with an unscoped business case — now the most common miss of the day. The pair spends Exercise 3 proving the problem is Android, then walks into Exercise 4 and prices a point at the all-users $884. The analysis says 17,500 people; the invoice says 32,000. It is the same error as quoting a gap with no n, one exercise later and with money attached, and it survives review because nobody re-reads the denominator once it is a dollar sign. The error runs both ways and the mirror image is worth the same mark-down: a pair that scoped its diagnosis to Android and then pitches a rebuild for everyone at $483 a point has undersold the strongest bet on the board and may lose it at the funding meeting.
  • Rebuilding a commodity without saying the word "vendor". Initiative ① — auto-capture, glare and edge detection, retry with a reason, compression — is line for line what Onfido, Veriff, Sumsub and Persona sell as a drop-in SDK. Building it yourself is a perfectly good answer; not having considered it is not. Ask: "what does the vendor charge per verification, and how many weeks is the integration?" The usual honest answer in regulated fintech is buy the capture, build the recovery flow around it — the second is your product, the first is not.

Push here

  • "Which Ex-3 evidence does this initiative attack?" · "Do the lifts add up to the target — and do any two of them rescue the same user?"
  • "How will you know it worked?" · "What must not move?" 2–3 pairs pitch their ranked list.
  • "A point of what, measured on whom?" Ask it the moment a dollar figure appears, and ask it of the pair with the most confident number. Follow with: "how many people are in that population, and is it the same population your Exercise 3 evidence came from?" If the two answers differ, the plan is priced wrong — and which direction it is wrong in is the interesting part.
  • "What does this cost, and when does it pay back?" · "Could you buy this instead of building it?" · "Is your month-one number the same as your steady-state number?" (It should not be — a lift stacks over the customer's life before it plateaus. On Nimbus the whole plan is worth $6.5k in month one and $17.7k a month by month three.)
5

Draft the client story

30 min (22 draft + 8 share-out)
A tight, numbers-first narrative that earns the "yes".

What good looks like

  • 6–8 slide arc: Situation → Evidence → Diagnosis → Plan → Impact → Ask. Evidence pulled from the dashboard. Grade the arc, not the count. Six beats, and evidence can honestly take two or three slides — the model client deck you reveal next runs nine content slides plus a cover for exactly that reason, so do not mark a pair down for a ninth slide that is carrying an argument. Mark them down for a sixth that is carrying a logo.
  • Impact stated in users AND money, with the month named: +6,400 verified → +3,360 activations/mo ≈ +$6.5k in month one, reaching ≈ +$18k/mo once about three months of cohorts have stacked up. One specific ask.
  • The impact number is attached to the population it came from. "+6,400 verified" is a claim about all 32,000 sign-ups. A pair whose Exercise 3 evidence was Android-only has to do one of two honest things on this slide: widen the fix and say why it reaches everyone (bet ① is a rebuild for all users, so it does), or shrink the number to the 17,500 it actually covers. What they must not do is diagnose 17,500 and bill for 32,000 — that is the slide a client's finance lead unpicks in ten seconds, and once one number is caught the whole deck is re-read as marketing.
  • The ask carries a number and a date. "Two engineers and a designer for the quarter" is a request; "37 person-weeks, about $148k, paying back roughly 10 months after it ships" is a decision someone can make. Strong pairs also name the two assumptions they are most likely to be wrong about — the loaded weekly rate and the value per point — and invite the client to attack those rather than the diagnosis.
  • The capacity line names what stops. "Three people for a quarter" sounds like 39 person-weeks (3 × 13) against 37 needed, and that spare 2 is a comfort number: it assumes no holiday, no on-call, no week-zero revert, and — the part nobody says out loud — three people who are currently doing something else. Read realistically it is 37 against about 34. A plan is not funded out of a budget, it is funded out of a roadmap, so a strong pair says which piece of work slips (on Nimbus: the merchant-rewards pilot, by a quarter) and, if two weeks go missing, which bet drops first and why — bet ④, last on ICE and last on payback at $0.98 per $1. The model client deck does exactly this; compare theirs against it. A capacity line that does not name what stops is not an ask, it is an assumption.
  • The question the dashboard cannot answer is answered as a plan, not a number. Asked how much of the 60% is compliance saying no on purpose, the funnel does not split it — "photo rejected / app crash on upload" is one bucket of 11,712, merging a policy decline with a glare rejection and a failed upload. A strong pair says so, then gives the floor the data does support: compliance rules do not know which handset you are holding, so the platform gap cannot be policy — Android has 7,900 of 15,800 photos accepted (50%) against iOS 9,508 of 13,320 (71%), and closing that gap alone is ≈3,400 more accepted photos and ≈2,200 more verified users a month that no regulator is refusing. Then what they would instrument and when: a reason code on the rejection event (policy / image quality / technical, terminal or retried) plus one conversation with risk & legal. "I don't know yet, and here is how I'd know by Friday" beats any percentage invented on the spot.

Common misses

  • Burying or omitting the ask; impact only in % (no money); a feature list instead of a story; hype adjectives.
  • Quoting a compliance share off the dashboard. "Maybe 15% of it is compliance" cannot come from this data — no screen in the kit separates a policy decline from a blurry photo. It sounds like rigour, and it is the one number in the pitch that is pure invention; a client whose risk team knows the real figure will notice within a sentence.
  • Quoting +$18k/month as if it lands immediately. The tell is $35,340 ÷ 6,720 = $5.26 "per activated user". That divides a monthly flow of revenue by a monthly flow of new activations, rather than by the stock of users actually generating it — and dividing a flow by a flow instead of by its stock silently multiplies the answer by the average lifetime, so what comes out is a lifetime value, not a monthly one. The monthly figure is $35,340 ÷ the 18,400 activated base = $1.92: that denominator is the stock, the people on the books this month, which is what a monthly revenue number is actually earned from. Both numbers are useful; the mistake is using the lifetime one as a run-rate. That 2.7 is not a coincidence — the stock divided by the flow that feeds it, 18,400 activated users ÷ 6,720 new per month, is the average customer life in months, which is both the ratio between the two figures and the number of months a monthly gain keeps stacking before it plateaus. Be exact about what it overstates, because the correction is not symmetric. Month one is overstated 2.7×. Year one is overstated by only about 8% — $212k claimed against $196k real on our plan — because with a 2.7-month ramp ten of the twelve months are already at plateau. A pair that "corrects" the annual figure by dividing it by 2.7 has made the mirror-image error and will lose the room to the first person who checks it. The ramp wrecks the launch-month claim and barely touches the annual one; knowing which of the two is on the slide is the entire skill. If a group defends $5.26 as "the right denominator, unlike ARPU", they have learned the wrong lesson confidently — that is worth stopping the room for.

Push here

  • "Read me your Situation and your Ask." · "What exactly do you want them to approve?" · "Where's the money?"
  • Make the sceptic ask all six: "convince me the Android drop isn't broken tracking", "how much of that 60% is compliance saying no on purpose?", "your lifts land exactly on the gap — which one bet do you want the money for?", "what does this cost me, and when do I get it back?", "that +6,400 — is that everyone, or the group you actually diagnosed?", and "if I say yes, what doesn't get built?"
  • The sixth is the one that separates a product manager from an analyst. Nobody has infinite engineers, so every "yes" is also a "no" to something — and a pair that cannot say what the no is has not actually asked for anything. Push twice: first "what stops?", then "and if you lose two weeks to on-call, what's the first thing you drop?" The second answer is where the ICE ranking earns its keep.
  • The fourth is the one most rooms cannot answer at all, which is why it is worth insisting on. A pair that cannot cost its plan does not have a presentation problem; it has an unfinished plan, and this room is a far cheaper place to find that out than the client's.
  • The second is worth insisting on for the opposite reason: there is no answer on the screen, and what you are grading is whether they say so and then hand you a method and a date. If a pair produces a confident share, ask where they read it — the silence that follows teaches more than the question did.
  • Then reveal the model client deck and compare — praise where theirs is sharper.
Running the room

Facilitation tips

  • Everything runs solo → pair → debrief, in fixed pairs for the whole day. This is the one instruction worth being rigid about. The solo block is short, silent and always the first thing a facilitator cuts when time slips — cut the debrief instead. Two people who have each formed a view independently bring roughly twice the hypotheses of two people who talked from minute one, and the pair phase only works if there are two positions to reconcile. Pair them across function and seniority, and have the non-typist present.
  • Turn on the dashboard's Hard mode for Exercises 2–3 so pairs diagnose instead of reading the answer off a red bar. Turn it off for your live tour. Easiest way: share nimbus-analytics.html?mode=hard as the link — the mode is remembered on that device however it was set, so nobody has to be talked through finding a toggle. The workbook and tracker links carry it onward, so a pair cannot click through into soft mode and read the answer off the banner.
  • Demo the comparison bar yourself, once, before Exercise 3 — ninety seconds, no more. + Add comparison → Platform is Android; every report on every view re-splits. Then ＋ and… on the same chip to add Country is Moldova, and say the sentence out loud: "now I am holding platform still and asking whether country still matters." Do not demo it on the country trap itself — that is theirs to find. Two things to flag while you have the room: the comparison follows you between views until you clear it, which is the point and also the commonest cause of a pair misreading a later chart; and a report that cannot honestly be split says so in a banner rather than inventing a number, so a missing split is information, not a bug.
  • The tracker's prefill is yours, not theirs — open it as initiative-tracker.html?facilitator=1. The ✨ Prefill the Nimbus worked example chip drops the finished Exercise-4 plan — objective, four initiatives, every score, every week estimate, every expected lift — straight into whatever plan is open, so it ships hidden and only that link reveals it. Pairs can still read the model answer any time on the 📘 Worked example tab; what they cannot do is start from it and call it theirs, which is the difference between seeing a good plan and having built one. Use the prefill on the projector at the debrief, and use ?facilitator=0 on a laptop a group will sit down at afterwards — like hard mode, the flag is remembered on that device once set.
  • In the workbook, give each pair a name and its own entry in the group switcher — a "group" in the switcher is one pair. Otherwise two pairs sharing a browser overwrite each other.
  • Keep the mock app's facilitator panel hidden while pairs explore (there's a "Hide" button).
  • Protect the time on Exercises 3 and 4 — cut debriefs before you cut work.
  • Watch the four traps all day: blended averages (always segment), solving off a number with no qual, a one-dimension gap believed before anything was held still (the country trap is the built-in example), and a rate quoted with no population under it — no n in Exercise 3, no denominator on the money in Exercise 4.
  • Land the meta-point: all data is synthetic, but the loop — journey → funnel → bottleneck → initiatives → story — works on any product they own.
